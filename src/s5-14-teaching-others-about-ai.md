# 5.14 Teaching others about AI
<!-- original: ch. 108 -->

You know something useful. Someone near you does not. A parent. A friend. A colleague. Teaching them is the kindest thing you can do with your AI skills. It is also the best way to deepen your own understanding. You learn twice when you teach once.

Teaching AI is hard because AI is strange. It is not a car or a cooker. People have no mental picture of it. Your job is to give them one. Start with their problem, not with the technology. People learn when the lesson solves something they care about.

## The golden rule: start with their problem

Ask what they struggle with. Long emails. Boring reports. Planning meals. Sorting photos. Pick one problem. Solve it together, live, in front of them. A solved problem is worth a hundred slides.

Do not start with a lecture on how AI works. Start with a win. "Watch this. I will turn your messy notes into a clean email in ten seconds." Their eyes will widen. Now they want to learn. Curiosity is the best teacher. A lecture is the worst.

Keep the first lesson to one thing. One tool. One task. One win. If you teach five tools at once, they will remember none. If you teach one tool well, they will come back for the second.

> NOTE: Never teach on someone else's private data. Use example material, not their real emails, photos, or bank letters. Privacy matters most when you are helping.

## An analogy bank for explaining AI

Analogies make strange ideas familiar. An analogy is a comparison that says "this new thing is like that known thing". Here is a bank you can borrow from.

A chatbot is like a very well-read intern. It knows a lot. It works fast. It needs clear instructions. And you must check its work. That one analogy covers most of what a beginner needs.

A prompt is like a recipe. Clear steps and good ingredients give a good result. Vague steps give a mess. Change one ingredient and the result changes.

RAG is like an open-book exam. The AI answers with the book open in front of it. That is why it knows your files.

An agent is like a personal assistant with a to-do list. It can plan steps and use tools. But it still needs you to check the big decisions.

A model is like a chef who learned from a million cookbooks. It can cook almost anything. It has never tasted your food. Your feedback teaches it your taste.

Pick the analogy that fits the person. A cook likes the chef. A manager likes the intern. A student likes the open-book exam. Meet people where they are.

## Meeting people where they are

People come in four types. Each needs a different approach.

The curious ones want to play. Give them a fun task. Write a poem. Make a picture. Plan a dream trip. Fun first, work later.

The sceptical ones want proof. Show them a real job done well. Show them the mistakes too. Honesty wins sceptics. Hype loses them.

The worried ones want safety. Talk about privacy. Show them what not to share. Show them the settings. Safety first, then skills.

The busy ones want time saved. Show them one task that takes ten minutes today and two minutes with AI. Time is their language. Speak it.

Watch for fear. Some people fear AI will take their job. Some fear looking stupid. Be kind. Say that everyone starts confused. Show your own mistakes. "Look, it got this wrong too. Let me fix it." A teacher who admits mistakes is a teacher people trust.

## Workshop outlines, and culture at work

A good workshop is short and hands-on. Here is a one-hour outline you can copy. Ten minutes on what AI is, with the intern analogy. Ten minutes on safety, with the three rules: no secrets, check facts, stay in charge. Thirty minutes of doing, with one task each person cares about. Ten minutes of sharing, where each person shows what they made.

A half-day workshop adds more. A session on prompts. A session on AI in their own apps. A session on what AI gets wrong. End with a personal plan. Each person writes down one thing they will try this week. Plans beat notes.

Building an AI-friendly culture at work means making it safe to try. Share wins in a team channel. Share mistakes too. Write a short team guide. What tools are approved? What data is off-limits? Who do you ask for help? A culture where people can ask dumb questions is a culture where people learn fast.

Measure whether it worked. Did people try the tool this week? Did they save time? Did they feel more confident? Ask them. A short survey after a workshop tells you more than applause does. If scores are low, change the workshop. Teaching is a loop, not a performance.

## Check yourself

1. What is the golden rule of teaching AI?
**Answer:** Start with the person's problem, not with the technology, and solve one real thing together.

2. Why use an analogy when explaining AI?
**Answer:** Because analogies give people a familiar mental picture for a strange new thing, like a well-read intern for a chatbot.

3. How do you teach a sceptical person?
**Answer:** Show them a real job done well, show the mistakes too, and let honesty win them over instead of hype.

4. What does a good one-hour workshop look like?
**Answer:** Ten minutes on what AI is, ten on safety, thirty of hands-on doing, and ten of sharing what each person made.

5. How do you know if your teaching worked?
**Answer:** Ask whether people tried the tool, saved time, and felt more confident, and change the workshop if scores are low.

## In short

- Teach from the other person's problem, and give them one win in the first lesson.
- Use analogies like the well-read intern, the recipe, and the open-book exam.
- Meet each person where they are: curious, sceptical, worried, or busy.
- Keep workshops short and hands-on, and end with a personal plan.
- Build a work culture where asking questions is safe, and measure what worked.
- Try this week: teach one person, try two analogies, answer one fear, and draft a workshop.
