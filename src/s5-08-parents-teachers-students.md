# 5.8 Parents, teachers, and students
<!-- original: ch. 98 -->

AI has entered homes and classrooms. Children meet it early. Parents worry about it. Teachers adapt to it. Students use it daily. This chapter is for all three groups. It gives honest rules for kids and homework, and practical ideas for learning together.

The big idea is simple. AI is a tool for learning, not a shortcut around learning. A student who uses AI to understand will learn faster. A student who uses AI to skip the work will learn nothing and get caught. The rules below protect everyone.

## For parents: a healthy AI household

Start with a conversation, not a ban. Ask your child what AI tools they use and what they use them for. Listen first. Most children will tell you if they are not afraid of punishment. A ban without a talk just moves the use underground.

Set clear house rules together. Write them down. Here is a starter set you can adapt. No AI for final homework answers. AI may explain, quiz, and check, but the child writes the work. No sharing personal details with AI tools. No photos, addresses, school names, or passwords. No AI chats with strangers. Check age limits on every tool. Many AI tools require users to be thirteen or older. Respect that limit.

Supervise young children directly. Sit with them while they use AI. Ask what the AI said. Check it together. For older children, check in weekly. What did you use AI for? What did you learn? What went wrong? A weekly check-in beats secret monitoring.

Model good use yourself. If your child sees you asking AI to write your emails and calling it done, they will do the same with homework. If they see you checking AI answers and thinking aloud, they will copy that too. Children copy what you do, not what you say.

> NOTE: Age limits exist for a reason. Many AI tools require users to be thirteen or older, and some require eighteen. Check the rules for each tool, and do not help a young child lie about their age to sign up.

## For students: learn with AI, not around it

Here is the honest rule for students. AI may help you learn. AI may not do your learning for you. Those two sentences cover almost everything.

Good uses are clear. Ask AI to explain a hard idea in plain words. Ask it to quiz you before a test. Ask it to check your draft for grammar and clarity. Ask it to help you plan an essay. Ask it for practice questions. Ask it to explain why your answer was wrong. Each of these makes you smarter.

Bad uses are also clear. Pasting an essay question into AI and handing in the answer. Asking AI to do your maths homework line by line. Using AI to write a lab report about an experiment you did not do. These are cheating. Teachers can often tell. AI writing has a flat, generic sound. And even if you are not caught, you have learned nothing. The exam will find out.

Learn to cite AI use honestly. If your school allows AI help, say where you used it. "I used AI to check my grammar" is honest. "I used AI to brainstorm ideas" is honest. Hiding AI use is dishonest. Ask your teacher what their rules are. Rules differ by school and subject. Follow your school's rules first. This book's advice comes second.

> TRY IT: Students, take one homework question you find hard. Ask AI to explain the idea, not the answer. Then close the AI, and write the answer yourself. Compare the result with asking AI for the answer directly. The first way teaches you. The second way only finishes the task.

## Homework help for parents

Parents often help with homework. AI changes how that works. Here is a routine that helps without doing the work for the child.

First, let the child try alone. Struggle is part of learning. Second, ask the child to explain the problem in their own words. If they cannot, the problem is understanding, not effort. Third, ask AI together to explain the idea with an example. Fourth, let the child try again. Fifth, check the answer together. Praise the effort, not just the result.

Do not let AI become the parent. If AI answers every question, the child stops asking you. Keep some homework time AI-free. Talk. Argue a little. Laugh. The relationship matters more than the worksheet.

Watch for signs of overuse. If homework is finished in seconds but the child cannot explain it, AI did it. If the child panics without AI, they depend on it. The fix is small and steady. One AI-free task a day. One explanation out loud. Confidence grows back fast.

## AI in the classroom

Teachers can use AI to save hours. Lesson plans. Quiz questions. Differentiated worksheets. Differentiated means different versions for different levels. AI can draft three versions of one worksheet in minutes. The teacher checks and fixes them. That is a real time saver.

Teachers can also teach AI literacy directly. Show students how AI works. Show them how it invents facts. Show them how to check a claim. A lesson on spotting AI errors is worth more than a lesson on writing prompts.

Set classroom rules clearly. Say what AI use is allowed and what is not. Say how to cite it. Say what happens if rules are broken. Clear rules are kinder than vague ones. Students follow rules they understand.

For older students and university, the stakes rise. Essays, dissertations, and exams have strict rules. Using AI against the rules can mean failing a course or losing a degree. Read the rules for each assignment. When in doubt, ask the teacher. Asking is always safer than guessing.

Family fun with AI is the light side. Make up stories together. Invent a board game. Write a song about your dog. Plan a treasure hunt. Draw a picture from a silly prompt. AI is at its best when a family plays with it together. The learning happens without anyone noticing.

## In short

- AI is a tool for learning, not a shortcut around learning.
- House rules: no AI for final answers, no personal details, respect age limits.
- Students may use AI to explain, quiz, and check, but must write their own work.
- Parents should help with a five-step routine and keep some homework time AI-free.
- Teachers can save hours on planning and teach students to spot AI errors.
- Try this week: a family talk, a ten-minute quiz, one homework routine, and one AI-checked lesson.
