# 3.7 NotebookLM: talk to your notes
<!-- original: ch. 76 -->

This chapter has one big change you must know first. The tool was called NotebookLM. On July 16, 2026, Google renamed it Gemini Notebook. Same tool. New name. This chapter uses the new name, Gemini Notebook. You may still see the old name in old articles, videos, and screenshots. They mean the same thing.

Gemini Notebook is a Google tool for talking to your own sources. You drop in your notes, PDFs, web pages, or videos. Then you ask questions. The answers come from your sources, with citations you can click. It is the friendliest RAG tool in this book. You do not write code. You do not build anything. You just add sources and chat.

Think of it as a notebook that reads itself back to you. You feed it the material. It becomes an expert on that material, and nothing else. It will not answer from general knowledge when your sources can answer. That limit is its strength.

## Why it is special

Most chatbots answer from what they learned in training. Gemini Notebook answers from your files. That difference changes everything. The answers are grounded. Grounded means tied to real sources you can check. Every answer shows the passages it came from. Click one, and you see the exact text.

It also makes audio. The famous feature is called Audio Overview. It turns your sources into a spoken conversation between two hosts. It sounds like a podcast about your notes. You can listen while you walk or drive. There is also a video version that turns sources into a narrated slide show. Both are generated from your material, not from the open web.

The Studio is where these things live. The Studio is the panel that turns your sources into outputs. Audio Overviews, study guides, briefing documents, timelines, and FAQs all come from here. Pick an output. Wait a minute or two. Get a polished result.

There is a limit you should know. Gemini Notebook works best with a focused set of sources. Ten to fifty sources is the sweet spot. A thousand scattered files will muddy the answers. Keep each notebook about one topic. Make a new notebook for each new topic.

## Chatting with your sources

Start by making a notebook. Give it a clear name, like "Biology exam" or "House renovation". Then add sources. You can upload PDFs, text files, and audio. You can paste web links. You can add YouTube links. You can paste text directly. Each source gets a title. You can turn sources on and off with a tick box. Off means the chat ignores that source.

Now ask a question. Ask it like you would ask a person who read everything. "What does the report say about costs?" is good. "Tell me everything" is bad. Specific questions get specific answers. Vague questions get vague answers.

Read the answer with its citations. Each claim has a small number. Click it to see the source passage. If the answer looks wrong, click through. The source is the truth. The answer is just a summary of it.

Use the suggested questions too. Gemini Notebook offers follow-up questions under each answer. They are often good. They show you angles you had not thought of. Click one to keep digging.

> TRY IT: Make a notebook with three sources: a short article, a page of your own notes, and a web link. Ask it a question that only one source can answer. Check the citation. Then ask a question none of the sources can answer. Notice what it says. A good answer admits the gap.

## Audio Overviews: the podcast machine

Audio Overview is the feature people tell their friends about. It takes your sources and makes a spoken show from them. Two AI hosts talk through the key ideas. They explain, joke a little, and connect the dots. The result feels like a podcast made just for you.

It is best for learning. Upload your course notes the night before a test. Listen to the overview on the bus. You will remember more than if you re-read the notes. It is also good for long reports. A fifty-page report becomes a ten-minute listen.

You can steer it. There is a way to focus the overview on one topic. Tell it to focus on the methods, or the results, or the arguments. You can also ask for a shorter or longer version, where the tool allows it. And you can join in. Some versions let you talk to the hosts while the show plays. Ask a question out loud. The hosts answer and carry on.

Be honest about the limits. The hosts can get a detail wrong. They can over-simplify. They are a starting point, not a source. If a fact matters, check it in the written source. Never quote the podcast in an essay. Quote the source it came from.

## The Studio: turn sources into anything

The Studio turns your notebook into useful outputs. Each one serves a different need.

A briefing document is a short summary of all your sources. It is the fastest way to get the big picture. A study guide lists key terms, questions, and answers. It is built for revision. A timeline pulls out dates and events in order. It is perfect for history or project work. An FAQ lists the questions people ask most, with answers from your sources. A table of contents maps the whole notebook at a glance.

Use them in order. Start with the briefing document. Read it. Then make a study guide. Then ask the chat follow-up questions about anything unclear. Then listen to the Audio Overview. That sequence turns a pile of files into real understanding.

## Workflows: studying, research, work, and life

For studying, upload your lecture notes, textbook chapters, and past papers. Make a study guide. Ask the chat to quiz you. Answer out loud, then ask it to mark your answers. Ask it to explain anything you got wrong in plain words. Repeat the weak topics the next day.

For research and writing, upload your sources before you write a word. Ask for the main arguments on each side. Ask for quotes with citations. Ask what is missing. Draft your piece yourself. Use the notebook to check facts, not to write for you. Your voice stays yours.

For work, keep a notebook per project. Upload meeting notes, plans, and reports. Before a meeting, ask for a two-minute briefing. After a meeting, add the new notes and ask what changed. The notebook becomes your project memory.

For life, keep a notebook for a big decision. A house move. A career change. A medical question. Upload the relevant documents. Ask for a comparison table. Ask for the risks. Remember the rule from Chapters 5.6 and 5.7. AI is not a financial adviser or a doctor. Use it to organise information. Make the decision with a real human expert.

> NOTE: Do not upload private medical records, legal papers, or anything you would not want stored on Google's servers. Read the privacy settings first. Delete notebooks you no longer need.

## Limits and tips

Every tool has limits. Gemini Notebook has a cap on how many sources fit in one notebook, and on how big each source can be. Check the current limits on Google's site. They change. If a source is too big, split it into chapters and upload the chapters.

Very long videos and audio files take a while to process. Upload them early. Short, focused sources work better than one giant file. A clear title on each source helps you find it later.

In your wider toolkit, Gemini Notebook sits between your notes app and your chatbot. Your notes app holds everything. Gemini Notebook holds one topic at a time, for deep work. Your chatbot handles open questions about the world. Use each tool for what it is best at.
