# 5.1 AI for research and learning
<!-- original: ch. 91 -->

Learning anything faster is the most useful AI skill there is. Research used to mean hours in a library. Now an AI can summarise a paper, quiz you on it, and explain the hard parts in plain words. Used well, it makes you smarter. Used badly, it makes you feel smart while you learn nothing. This chapter shows the good way.

Research means finding reliable facts and understanding them. Learning means turning those facts into knowledge you keep. AI helps with both. It finds sources fast. It explains ideas simply. It tests you until the ideas stick. But it cannot do the understanding for you. That part is still yours.

## The research tool lineup

Different jobs need different tools. Here is the lineup in plain words.

A general chatbot is your starting point. It explains ideas and brainstorms angles. It is fast and flexible. It is also the most likely to invent a fact. Treat its claims as leads, not sources.

A search-first assistant is better for facts. Tools like Perplexity search the live web and show sources with every claim. Use these when you need current facts with links. Always click the links. A source you did not open is a source you did not check.

A deep research tool goes further. It reads many sources and writes a structured report. Google's Deep Research and similar tools do this. Give it a clear question. Wait a few minutes. Get a report with citations. This is the tool for big questions like "What do we know about this topic?"

Gemini Notebook holds your own sources and answers from them. It is covered in Chapter 3.7. Use it when you have a fixed set of papers or notes. It keeps you grounded in material you chose.

A tutor setup is different again. You ask the AI to act as a teacher. It explains one idea at a time. It asks you questions. It waits for your answer. It corrects you kindly. This is the best setup for learning, and the one most people skip.

> NOTE: AI can invent citations that look real. A fake paper title with a fake author is a common failure. Never put a citation in your work unless you have opened the source yourself.

## The 5-step research workflow

Good research follows a pattern. Use these five steps every time.

Step one is to frame the question. Write it down in one sentence. "How does sleep affect memory in teenagers?" is a question. "Tell me about sleep" is not. A sharp question gets a sharp answer.

Step two is to gather sources. Ask a search-first tool for the key papers, reports, or articles. Collect five to ten good ones. Skip anything without an author or a date. Skip anything that only quotes other articles.

Step three is to read with help. Upload the best sources to Gemini Notebook or paste them into a chat. Ask for a summary of each one. Then ask for the main argument, the evidence, and the limits. Limits are what the study did not prove. Honest research always names its limits.

Step four is to check sources like a pro. Ask three questions about each source. Who wrote it, and why should I trust them? When was it written, and is it still current? What evidence does it give, and could there be another explanation? If a source fails these checks, drop it. One strong source beats five weak ones.

Step five is to synthesise. Synthesis means combining sources into your own understanding. Write a short paragraph in your own words. What do the sources agree on? Where do they disagree? What is still unknown? If you cannot write that paragraph, you have not understood yet. Go back to step three.

> TRY IT: Pick a topic you know nothing about. Run the five steps with a one-hour limit. Frame one question. Gather five sources. Summarise them. Check two of them properly. Write one paragraph in your own words. You now know more about that topic than most people ever will.

## Learning techniques, AI-supercharged

AI makes classic study methods faster. Here are the ones that work best.

Active recall means testing yourself instead of re-reading. Ask the AI to quiz you on a topic. Answer from memory. Ask it to mark your answers and explain the gaps. Ten minutes of quizzing beats an hour of re-reading.

Spaced repetition means revisiting ideas at growing intervals. Today, then in three days, then in a week. Ask the AI to build you a set of flashcards. Review a few each day. The forgetting curve is real. Repetition at the right time flattens it.

The Feynman technique means explaining an idea in simple words, as if to a twelve-year-old. Ask the AI to play the twelve-year-old. Explain the idea to it. Ask it where your explanation was unclear. Rewrite until it is clear. If you cannot explain it simply, you do not understand it yet.

Interleaving means mixing topics instead of studying one block at a time. Study a little of topic A, then topic B, then back to A. It feels harder. It works better. Ask the AI to build a mixed practice set.

Elaboration means connecting new ideas to things you already know. Ask the AI for an analogy. "Explain this like a kitchen" or "explain this like a football team". A good analogy is a hook your memory can hold.

## Do not let AI make you dumber

There is a trap. If AI writes your essay, summarises your reading, and answers your questions, you learn nothing. The work is the learning. Outsourcing the work outsources the learning.

Keep the hard parts for yourself. Let AI explain. Let AI quiz. Let AI check. Do not let AI write the final answer you hand in. Do not let AI read the book for you and tell you what it said. Read the hard page yourself. Then use AI to check your understanding.

Watch for fluent nonsense. AI writes smoothly even when it is wrong. Smooth writing feels true. Check claims that matter, especially numbers, dates, and quotes. If a claim would embarrass you if wrong, verify it in the source.

Research prompts make a big difference. A good prompt names the job and the shape of the answer. Try these. "Summarise this paper in five bullets: question, method, result, limits, and why it matters." "List the three strongest arguments for and against this idea, with a source for each." "Explain this concept with one everyday example." "What would a sceptic say about this claim?" "Turn these notes into ten quiz questions, with answers on a separate list." Each prompt does one job. One job per prompt beats five jobs in one prompt. If the answer is weak, the prompt was vague. Sharpen the prompt and ask again.

Deep research tools deserve a closer look. They read many sources and write a structured report with citations. Give one a clear question. Wait a few minutes. Read the report. Then check the three most important claims in the original sources. A report is a map, not the land. The sources are the land.

## Check yourself

1. What is the difference between a general chatbot and a search-first assistant for research?
**Answer:** A general chatbot answers from training and may invent facts, while a search-first assistant searches the live web and shows sources with each claim.

2. What are the five steps of the research workflow?
**Answer:** Frame the question, gather sources, read with help, check sources like a pro, and synthesise in your own words.

3. What three questions should you ask about every source?
**Answer:** Who wrote it and why trust them, when was it written and is it current, and what evidence does it give.

4. What is active recall, and why does it beat re-reading?
**Answer:** Testing yourself from memory instead of re-reading, which forces your brain to retrieve ideas and makes them stick.

5. Why should you not let AI write your final answer?
**Answer:** Because the work is the learning, and outsourcing the writing outsources the understanding.

## In short

- Use the right tool: chatbot to explain, search-first for facts, deep research for big questions.
- Frame one sharp question before you start gathering sources.
- Check every source for author, date, and evidence, and open citations yourself.
- Study with active recall, spaced repetition, and simple explanations.
- Let AI explain, quiz, and check, but keep the real thinking for yourself.
- Try this week: one question, five sources, a ten-minute quiz, and one simple explanation.
