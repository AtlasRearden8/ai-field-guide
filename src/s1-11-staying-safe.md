# 1.11 Staying safe: privacy, scams, and deepfakes
<!-- original: ch. 11 -->

AI is useful. It is also powerful in the wrong hands. This chapter is your safety guide. It covers three things: what not to share, how scammers use AI, and what a deepfake is. The rules are simple. Learn them once, and they protect you for years.

## What not to share

Your chats feel private. They feel like talking to a friend. They are not a private conversation. Your words go to a company's computers. They may be stored. They may be read by staff for safety checks. They may be used to improve the model, unless you turn that off.

So follow one rule: **never put anything in a chatbot that you would not want a stranger to read.**

Do not share:

- Passwords, PINs, or security codes.
- Full bank account numbers or card numbers.
- Your full address, or your children's school and timetable.
- Passport numbers, ID numbers, or driving licence numbers.
- Photos of other people without their permission.
- Anything about someone else's health, money, or private life.

What can you share? General things. "I earn about £2,000 a month" is fine for a budget plan. Your exact salary, bank, and account number are not. Remove private numbers from documents before you upload them. Chapter 1.5 shows you how to turn off training on your chats. Do that if it matters to you.

> NOTE: If you paste something sensitive by accident, delete the chat. Deleting does not guarantee the company erases its copy at once, but it is still the right move, and most companies delete stored chats on a schedule.

## How scammers use AI

Scammers have always lied. AI makes their lies better. It writes perfect English. It copies voices. It makes fake videos. Here is what to watch for.

**The urgent message.** "Your account will be closed in 24 hours." "Your parcel is stuck. Pay now." "Your grandchild is in trouble. Send money." Urgency is the scammer's favourite tool. Real companies do not rush you like this.

**The familiar voice.** AI can now copy a person's voice from a short recording. A scammer can call you sounding like your child, your boss, or your bank. If a call asks for money or a code, stop. Hang up. Call the person back on a number you already have.

**The perfect email.** AI writes flawless emails with no spelling mistakes. The old advice, "look for bad spelling", no longer works. Look instead for the request: money, a password, a link you did not expect, or a file you did not ask for.

**The too-good offer.** A job that pays a fortune for no work. A prize you never entered. A cheap phone from a stranger. If it feels too good, it is a trick.

## Your accounts are the target

Scammers do not only want your money. They want your accounts. Your email, your bank, your social media. Once they have one, they can reset the others.

Protect them with three habits.

**One password per account.** If you use the same password everywhere, one leak opens every door. Use a password manager. It makes a strong password for each site and remembers them all. You only remember one master password.

**Two-step verification everywhere.** Turn it on for your email first, then your bank, then your social media. Your email is the master key. If someone gets into your email, they can reset your other passwords. Protect it best.

**Know how recovery works.** If you lose your phone, how do you get back into your accounts? Set up a recovery email or phone number now, while you are calm. Write down your backup codes and keep them somewhere safe, not on your phone.

> NOTE: No real company will ever ask for your password or your two-step code by phone, email, or chat. Anyone who asks is a scammer, even if they sound official.

If you think an account has been hacked, act fast. Change the password from a device you trust. Turn on two-step verification. Check for strange messages sent from your account. Tell your bank if money is involved. Speed matters more than embarrassment.

## The STOP rule

When something feels urgent, strange, or too good, use the STOP rule.

- **S — Stop.** Do not click, do not pay, do not reply.
- **T — Think.** Would the real person or company ask this, in this way?
- **O — Observe.** Check the sender's address, the phone number, the link. Look closely. Scammers use addresses that are almost right.
- **P — Phone.** Contact the person or company on a number you already have, not the one in the message.

One phone call to a known number defeats almost every scam.

## What is a deepfake?

A **deepfake** is a fake photo, video, or voice made by AI to look or sound real. The word comes from "deep learning" and "fake". A deepfake can put words in a real person's mouth, or put a real person's face on a fake video.

Deepfakes are used for scams, for fake news, and for embarrassing people. They are getting better every year. But they still leave clues.

**How to spot a deepfake:**

- The lips do not quite match the words.
- The eyes blink oddly, or not at all.
- The hands look wrong: too many fingers, or fingers that bend strangely.
- The lighting on the face does not match the background.
- The voice sounds flat, or the breathing sounds wrong.
- The video is short, blurry, or cropped oddly, which hides mistakes.

No single clue proves a fake. Two or three together should make you suspicious. And the best test is not visual at all. It is the STOP rule: check with the real person, through a channel you trust.

> TRY IT: Search for "how to spot a deepfake" and look at two or three examples. Notice the hands, the eyes, and the lip sync. Once you have seen a few, you will spot them faster. Then tell one family member what you learned. That conversation protects two people.

## Protecting your family

Talk about this chapter with your family. Especially with older relatives and children.

- **Agree a family code word.** If someone calls claiming to be a family member in trouble, ask for the code word. A scammer will not know it.
- **Teach children the sharing rules.** No names, addresses, school names, or photos of friends in a chatbot.
- **Check privacy settings together.** Turn off chat training and memory on shared devices if you prefer.
- **Make it okay to ask.** The best defence against scams is a family where nobody is embarrassed to say "I got a strange message. Can you look at it?"

**Answer:** Passwords or PINs, full bank or card numbers, and ID or passport numbers. Also addresses, school details, and photos of others without permission.

**Answer:** Stop, Think, Observe, Phone. Do not click or pay. Think if it is real. Check the sender closely. Call the person or company on a number you already have.

**Answer:** A fake photo, video, or voice made by AI to look or sound like a real person.

## In short

- Treat chats as semi-public. Never share passwords, bank details, or ID numbers.
- Remove private numbers from documents before you upload them.
- Scammers use AI for urgent messages, copied voices, and perfect emails.
- Urgency is the warning sign. Use the STOP rule.
- Deepfakes are fake videos and voices. Check hands, eyes, and lip sync, then verify with the real person.
- Agree a family code word, and make it okay to ask for help.
